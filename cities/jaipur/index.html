What the page finds

  • PM2.5 is behind 669 to 6,094 deaths a year in Jaipur, depending on the risk curve and the map: lowest on Brown 2022 (log-linear) with the AQLI satellite, highest on Jaganathan 2024 (log-linear) with the CPCB monitors (8 curves and 3 maps tried, none preferred).

  • The first measure with a full cost is Retiring older trucks and buses: 0.63 to 19.4 expected life-years per crore across the curves and source studies (lowest on Brown 2022 (log-linear), highest on Jaganathan 2024 (log-linear)).
  • The top measures with partial costs (HEPA purifiers in classrooms (all-India school figures); Free HEPA purifiers for homes) are not comparable with the fully costed ones; each says what its cost leaves out.

This page follows the air of one city, Jaipur, from the ground to what could be done about it. It is written to understand, not to persuade. Where published sources disagree, the page shows them side by side and does not average them or pick a winner.

It has four chapters. 1. The air itself: what the monitors on the ground and the satellite maps say about PM2.5, the fine particles (smaller than 2.5 micrometres across) that do most of the harm to health, measured in micrograms per cubic metre of air (µg/m³). 2. Where it comes from: what the studies of the city's air say the particles came from. 3. What it does to health: the deaths and years of life that PM2.5 takes, on every published way of turning air into health. 4. What could work, and what it would buy: measures the city could take, with the odds each works and what it would cost.

Which air reading each chapter uses. Chapters 1 to 3 start from the CPCB monitors, the readings taken on the ground, and show the satellite maps beside them. Chapter 4 scores measures on the ACAG satellite map, the one the ranking across cities uses: the ranking compares cities, so it needs one map that covers every city the same way, and monitors exist for only some of them.

The air itself

This chapter is about what the air holds, measured two ways. The government's ground monitors (the Central Pollution Control Board, CPCB, and the state boards that run them) read the air at a fixed spot every hour. Satellite maps estimate it everywhere from space. The main pollutant here is PM2.5: particles under 2.5 micrometres across, small enough to reach deep into the lungs. It is counted in µg/m³, micrograms of particles in a cubic metre of air. An annual mean is the average over a whole year.

Where it comes from

A source study asks what the particles in a city's air came from. There are two kinds. An emissions model starts from what is burned (a list of vehicles, chimneys, fires) and computes where the smoke goes. A receptor model starts from what landed on a filter and works backwards to the likely sources. They answer slightly different questions and they disagree. They are shown side by side here, not averaged.

What it does to health

This chapter turns the air into health. A risk curve is a published estimate of how much the chance of dying rises as the yearly PM2.5 level rises. An exposure map says what level people in a place breathe. Put the two together and you get the deaths attributable to the air: the deaths a year that would not happen if the air were clean down to the curve's floor, the level below which the curve counts no further harm. Life-years add up the years of life those deaths took, each death counted by the years the person had left. Life expectancy gained is how much longer the average person would live.

The curves do not agree, and neither do the maps. The PM2.5 level for Jaipur is CPCB monitors 51.0 µg/m³ (2025); AQLI satellite 44.3 µg/m³ (2023); ACAG satellite 49.4 µg/m³ (2023). Across every curve and map below, the deaths a year attributable to the air run from 669 to 6,094. The gap comes from the curves (each was fitted to different studies, some mostly of cleaner air than India's) and from the maps (they measure the air differently). No curve or map is picked here, and nothing is averaged.

How these figures were made

The notes below apply to every table above, one note for each exposure map (each says which map it is for).

Map: cpcb 2025 (CPCB ground monitors, city annual means). Deaths for gbd2019, gbd2023, gemm_5cod: the GBD 2023 round's death rates by state, cause and age for 2023 (the nearest year in the files to 2025) × this tool's population, not scaled to the SRS; their share of deaths is of the all-cause count above. Ladakh takes GBD's Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh, and Andaman & Nicobar, Chandigarh, Dadra & Nagar Haveli, Daman & Diu, Lakshadweep and Puducherry GBD's pooled Other Union Territories; gbd2019: GBD 2019-round deaths are no longer published, so the GBD 2023 round's are used. Population of Jaipur: Census 2011 urban agglomeration, not updated to 2025; deaths and life-years are low by however much the city has grown since. What could work, and what it would buy

A measure is one thing the city could do about its air, written as a file in the measure library with every number's source. There are two kinds. An air measure cuts a cause of PM2.5 (dust, vehicles, burning) and so cleans the outdoor air for everyone; how much it cleans depends on how big that cause is, which comes from a source study (chapter 2). An exposure measure (purifiers, masks) leaves the outdoor air as it is and cuts what some people breathe, for some of their hours.

Each measure has odds that it works: the chance it is carried out and does what it says, mostly a judgement written down with its reasons. Figures marked if it works assume it does; expected figures multiply the life-years by those odds (and the cost too, for the part paid only on success). Every figure is a Monte Carlo range: the measure is run many times with its uncertain inputs (how big the cut is, the curve's own parameters, the cost) drawn afresh each time; the median is the middle run and the range covers the middle 90% of runs. Life-years per crore is life-years gained for every crore (ten million rupees) spent: higher means more health for the money.

7 of the 22 library measures apply to Jaipur. The library is written from single-city evidence: each measure file takes one city's costs, who the measure reaches and the odds it works from sources about that place, and names the cities it is written for. A measure applies here only if its file names Jaipur or says it fits every city, or if it acts on a whole region that includes the city. Carrying a measure to a city its file does not name would mean inventing its numbers, and this page does not.

Of the measures that apply: none is written for Jaipur alone; written for several cities or for all of them, so applied here with whatever evidence they borrow from elsewhere (each says which): Free HEPA purifiers for homes; Retiring older trucks and buses; HEPA purifiers in classrooms (all-India school figures); acting on a whole region: Scrubbers (flue-gas desulphurisation) on India's coal power plants; Subsidising Happy Seeders and other stubble machines in Punjab, five more years; Paying Punjab and Haryana farmers to move 8 lakh hectares out of paddy; Paying Punjab's farmers not to burn stubble, part paid up front.

The other 15 do not apply. 15 are written for one other city each (Amritsar 1, Bengaluru 11, Delhi 1, Patna 1, Surat 1).

Every measure is scored on one exposure map, the ACAG satellite (ACAG V6.GL.02.04 total PM2.5, districts, WorldPop-weighted), where Jaipur's PM2.5, weighted by where people live, is 49.4 µg/m³ in 2023. Chapter 1's ground monitors read the city's air differently, and chapter 3 shows the maps side by side; this chapter uses the map the ranking across cities uses.

Air measures are scored on every source study of Jaipur that splits PM2.5 by cause (APnA 2015 (emissions model), McDuffie 2021 (global emissions model)), each on its own; the studies disagree, and their results are never combined. Exposure measures do not depend on a study and are scored once.

What the scores assume

  • Share times cut: cutting a cause of PM2.5 by a given percent is taken to cut its share of the city's PM2.5 by the same percent.
  • No chemistry: real air is not additive (particles formed in the air from gases, limits set by ammonia, the dome over a city), and none of that is modelled.
  • Health only: benefits to the climate, crops or time saved are not counted, and nothing is valued in money.
  • Life-years and costs are counted over the measure's years plus the years in which its effect on deaths keeps arriving (20 under the default lag), discounted at 3% a year; deaths postponed are not discounted.
  • Cost is what a government or funder would spend (the funder's rupees), in 2025 rupees; a measure that saves society money overall is listed apart, not ranked.
