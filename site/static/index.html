air apocalypse

Bengaluru's government monitors read 27.9 μg/m³ of PM2.5 through 2025. That is inside India's standard of 40 and 5.6 times the WHO guideline of 5. Eight published risk curves turn that number into deaths, and they return between 1,577 and 11,155 a year.

The figure is one command. The model is a command-line tool, open, with every input’s source beside it.

Run it yourselfuvx --from 'git+https://github.com/dogluk/air-apocalypse#subdirectory=health' airhealth burden Bengaluru

7.1 times apart on the toll, one order of what to do

The model holds 22 measures against PM2.5. Each has what it would cut, the odds that it works and what it would cost a government or a funder, and each number was read at its source. 17 of them can be scored in Bengaluru.

A curve that counts more deaths counts more for every measure, so the dots slide together and the order holds. The first three places are the same on all eight curves, and no measure moves more than 3 places. The gaps between measures are far wider than the gaps between curves: an LPG refill subsidy buys 72 to 724 expected life-years per crore, a smog tower 0.012 to 0.12.

The two measures at the top are drawn hollow. The only published cost for each is the city’s enforcement budget, under a crore a year, so their ratios cannot be set beside the rest. The pale bands show where each lands once what builders and waste collection would spend is added. Dust controls at construction sites land at rank 1, at 97 to 974 life-years per crore. Ending open waste burning falls to rank 3, at 11 to 115, and to rank 4–7 if the cost sits at the top of its range. Those added costs rest partly on clearance budgets and on officials quoted in the press, which is why they are a what-if beside the default and not the default.

Among the 9 measures with a full cost by default, LPG refills come first on every curve and road paving second.

What changes the order

Not the risk curve. On each of 3 exposure maps and on either study of the city’s sources, the three measures that lead are the same on all eight curves. The study is another matter. CSTEP’s receptor study does not report waste burning or cooking fuel, so the waste-burning ban and LPG refills cannot be scored on it at all, and a different three lead.

Which exposure map, which death counts and whether the future is discounted: none of them changes Bengaluru’s top 5 on any curve. How much paying farmers and subsidising machines really cuts stubble burning changes it on all eight, and how much paving cuts road dust on 5. Among the fully costed measures the order turns on which study of the city’s sources is right. The APnA model and CSTEP’s receptor study disagree enough to move a measure by 8 places.

Where the smoke goes

India has no open table of how much PM2.5 a tonne emitted in one district adds in another. For crop burning the model stands on a stand-in, a global model’s source shares multiplied by satellite PM2.5. It puts India’s exposure to crop-burning smoke at 0.48 μg/m³. Lan et al. 2022 put it at 6.7.

So the model has paying farmers not to burn at 1.4 times as good as scrubbers per life, where the working paper behind the payments trial has 83. The table is not scaled to close the gap. Every measure that acts on a whole region is understated until a better one exists.

As a what-if beside the default, with the table scaled to Lan’s figure, paying farmers not to burn moves from rank 4–5 to 3–4, at 89 to 1,060 life-years per crore.

One city, end to end

Bengaluru, from the air to what could work follows one city from the monitors to the measures: ten years of hourly readings, the satellite maps against the ground, every study of its sources side by side with what each cannot see, deaths on each curve, and each measure with its sources. The same page exists for 11 cities.

On winter evenings the monitors peak at 52 μg/m³ at 8 pm, against 32 at 2 pm. Through the monsoon the day runs between 16 and 24.

Fifty cities

The same eight curves run over fifty cities, from Delhi at 99 μg/m³ to Thiruvananthapuram at 23. India’s own standard is 40, and 36 of the fifty are above it.

The measures do not reach as far as the curves. 11 of the 22 are scored only in Bengaluru, 3 in every city, and 4 act on whole regions. The rest wait on a number for each city that nobody has published.

Open questions

The inputs that reorder the ranking are the ones worth paying to settle.

  • Where smoke goes. An open source-receptor table for India, by district, sector and season. It fixes the 14-fold gap above and every regional measure with it.
  • What the top two cost in full. Ending waste burning and controlling construction dust rank first on an enforcement budget. Someone has to count collection, compliance and treatment.
  • How much the stubble measures cut. Payments not to burn and subsidised machines, measured at scale against monitors.
  • How much paving and sweeping cut road dust, measured in an Indian city. The transport and dust effects here are borrowed from Delhi.
  • Which source study is right. One method, a model and a receptor study side by side, in two seasons, starting with Bengaluru.
  • Counts for the other forty-nine cities. Most measures are scored in one city because the per-city number they need, generator capacity or kiln counts, has not been published.

The repository keeps the full list: 63 open items, each with what to read, the file it lands in and the check that proves it.

Run it

alias airhealth="uvx --from 'git+https://github.com/dogluk/air-apocalypse#subdirectory=health' airhealth"
airhealth burden Bengaluru          # deaths on every curve, seconds
airhealth causes Bengaluru          # every source study, side by side
airhealth score Bengaluru           # each measure, with its flags
airhealth rank Bengaluru            # the ranking, one table per curve; about a quarter of an hour

To change it, clone it: git clone https://github.com/dogluk/air-apocalypse, then pip install -e "health[dev]".

The model never averages across curves, studies or maps, and a source a study does not report is not counted as zero. No specialist has reviewed the method yet. Corrections are the most useful thing you can send: open an issue.

The notebook

India’s air kills on the scale that tobacco killed in the twentieth century. The response has mostly been theatre: towers that clean a two-hundred-metre radius, aircraft seeding clouds that aren’t there.

This is an open notebook for the unglamorous part — measuring what’s actually happening, finding the analyses nobody has run, and working out which ambitious bets survive contact with arithmetic. Everything is reproducible from free data, and the code is public.

What Diwali does to the air

Ten years of hourly government monitor data for India’s ten largest cities, with the things that happen at the same time pulled apart: the start of winter smog season, crop-stubble smoke, and monsoon rain.

Every city has a real Diwali effect — about four times normal PM2.5 at midnight, and close to twelve times in Chennai. But the famous post-Diwali smog is mostly not Diwali. Adjusted for season, weather and fires, the week after is statistically indistinguishable from normal everywhere. What looks like a hangover is the smog season arriving on schedule.

Confounding runs in opposite directions across the country. In the north, Diwali lands as stubble burning peaks, so a naive comparison overstates it — Delhi falls from +66% to +32%. In the south, retreating monsoon rain washes the air, so a naive comparison understates it: Bengaluru rises from +19% to +35%.

There’s a twist in what people search for. Around Diwali, Indian searches for “air quality index” rise 237% and “air pollution” 36% — while searches for cough and sore throat don’t move at all. Burns rise 75%, so the method can see a real effect when one is there. Attention spikes; symptoms, at least as search reveals them, don’t.

The question behind all of it

concentration ≈ emissions ÷ (mixing height × ventilation)

Every intervention attacks one of those three terms, or else gives up on the air and protects the lungs directly. Sorting proposals by which term they touch kills most of them in a single step.

Two numbers do most of the work. Delhi’s smog weighs about 150 tonnes — the entire visible apocalypse over thirty million people is a few hundred tonnes of dust, which is why scrubbing feels tractable. And it refills in about a day. A perfect, free, instantaneous scrub of the whole city buys one clean day. That ratio kills smog towers, outdoor filtration, and most of what gets announced each November.

Winter cloud seeding fails for a reason worth stating precisely: at fifteen percent humidity, condensing every molecule of water in a kilometre-high column over Delhi yields 2.6 mm of rain. Real efficiency is a fraction of that — drizzle, which doesn’t scrub. It isn’t a technology limit. The water isn’t there.

What hasn’t been analysed

The most useful thing here may be a list of questions the data can answer that nobody has asked. Six of them, each with a confidence and a note on what would kill it — including whether firecracker bans ever worked (re-argued in the Supreme Court every autumn on almost no evidence, and answerable with a difference-in-differences across states that banned at different times), and how much monitor siting biases every Indian air quality number, including ours.
