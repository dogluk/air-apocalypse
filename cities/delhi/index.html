The air itself

This chapter is about what the air holds, measured two ways. The government's ground monitors (the Central Pollution Control Board, CPCB, and the state boards that run them) read the air at a fixed spot every hour. Satellite maps estimate it everywhere from space. The main pollutant here is PM2.5: particles under 2.5 micrometres across, small enough to reach deep into the lungs. It is counted in µg/m³, micrograms of particles in a cubic metre of air. An annual mean is the average over a whole year.

Where it comes from

A source study asks what the particles in a city's air came from. There are two kinds. An emissions model starts from what is burned (a list of vehicles, chimneys, fires) and computes where the smoke goes. A receptor model starts from what landed on a filter and works backwards to the likely sources. They answer slightly different questions and they disagree. They are shown side by side here, not averaged.

What it does to health

This chapter turns the air into health. A risk curve is a published estimate of how much the chance of dying rises as the yearly PM2.5 level rises. An exposure map says what level people in a place breathe. Put the two together and you get the deaths attributable to the air: the deaths a year that would not happen if the air were clean down to the curve's floor, the level below which the curve counts no further harm. Life-years add up the years of life those deaths took, each death counted by the years the person had left. Life expectancy gained is how much longer the average person would live.

The curves do not agree, and neither do the maps. The PM2.5 level for Delhi is CPCB monitors 94.3 µg/m³ (2025); AQLI satellite 88.3 µg/m³ (2023); ACAG satellite 98.8 µg/m³ (2023). Across every curve and map below, the deaths a year attributable to the air run from 5,898 to 41,270. The gap comes from the curves (each was fitted to different studies, some mostly of cleaner air than India's) and from the maps (they measure the air differently). No curve or map is picked here, and nothing is averaged.

What could work, and what it would buy

A measure is one thing the city could do about its air, written as a file in the measure library with every number's source. There are two kinds. An air measure cuts a cause of PM2.5 (dust, vehicles, burning) and so cleans the outdoor air for everyone; how much it cleans depends on how big that cause is, which comes from a source study (chapter 2). An exposure measure (purifiers, masks) leaves the outdoor air as it is and cuts what some people breathe, for some of their hours.

Each measure has odds that it works: the chance it is carried out and does what it says, mostly a judgement written down with its reasons. Figures marked if it works assume it does; expected figures multiply the life-years by those odds (and the cost too, for the part paid only on success). Every figure is a Monte Carlo range: the measure is run many times with its uncertain inputs (how big the cut is, the curve's own parameters, the cost) drawn afresh each time; the median is the middle run and the range covers the middle 90% of runs. Life-years per crore is life-years gained for every crore (ten million rupees) spent: higher means more health for the money.

8 of the 22 library measures apply to Delhi. The library is written from single-city evidence: each measure file takes one city's costs, who the measure reaches and the odds it works from sources about that place, and names the cities it is written for. A measure applies here only if its file names Delhi or says it fits every city, or if it acts on a whole region that includes the city. Carrying a measure to a city its file does not name would mean inventing its numbers, and this page does not.

Of the measures that apply: written for Delhi: Cloud seeding over Delhi in winter (long shot); written for several cities or for all of them, so applied here with whatever evidence they borrow from elsewhere (each says which): Free HEPA purifiers for homes; Retiring older trucks and buses; HEPA purifiers in classrooms (all-India school figures); acting on a whole region: Scrubbers (flue-gas desulphurisation) on India's coal power plants; Subsidising Happy Seeders and other stubble machines in Punjab, five more years; Paying Punjab and Haryana farmers to move 8 lakh hectares out of paddy; Paying Punjab's farmers not to burn stubble, part paid up front.

The other 14 do not apply. 14 are written for one other city each (Amritsar 1, Bengaluru 11, Patna 1, Surat 1).

Every measure is scored on one exposure map, the ACAG satellite (ACAG V6.GL.02.04 total PM2.5, districts, WorldPop-weighted), where Delhi's PM2.5, weighted by where people live, is 98.8 µg/m³ in 2023. Chapter 1's ground monitors read the city's air differently, and chapter 3 shows the maps side by side; this chapter uses the map the ranking across cities uses.

Air measures are scored on every source study of Delhi that splits PM2.5 by cause (ARAI-TERI Delhi 2018 (emissions model), ARAI-TERI Delhi 2018 (receptor study), ARAI-TERI Delhi 2018 (emissions model, by region), Biswal 2025, 2022 run (emissions model), Biswal 2025, 2023 run (emissions model), Biswal 2025, 2024 run (emissions model), Govardhan 2024 (emissions model, by region), Kulkarni 2020 (emissions model)), each on its own; the studies disagree, and their results are never combined. What the scores assume

  • Share times cut: cutting a cause of PM2.5 by a given percent is taken to cut its share of the city's PM2.5 by the same percent.
  • No chemistry: real air is not additive (particles formed in the air from gases, limits set by ammonia, the dome over a city), and none of that is modelled.
  • Health only: benefits to the climate, crops or time saved are not counted, and nothing is valued in money.
  • Life-years and costs are counted over the measure's years plus the years in which its effect on deaths keeps arriving (20 under the default lag), discounted at 3% a year; deaths postponed are not discounted.
  • Cost is what a government or funder would spend (the funder's rupees), in 2025 rupees; a measure that saves society money overall is listed apart, not ranked.

Cloud seeding over Delhi in winter (long shot)

winter_cloud_seeding.toml: exposure measure: cuts what some people breathe; the outdoor air is unchanged.

Who it reaches: everyone in Delhi, on the few winter days that have seedable cloud, if rain falls.

Odds that it works: 2%. Basis: judgement: the lowest in the library. Delhi's winter air holds too little water to rain out much (under 1 mm even if every molecule in a 1 km column condensed), seeding only triggers storms that winter does not have the energy to make, and forcing it means out-heating the sun (bets/README.md, 'Why winter cloud seeding can't work in Delhi'); the October 2025 trials gave no rain in Delhi (cloud moisture 10-20% against about 50% needed). Not zero because the trials were few and in one dry week.

PM2.5 change if it works, in what the covered people breathe (not the city average; everyone else breathes the same air as before): -0.49 µg/m³ (-0.61 to -0.36).
