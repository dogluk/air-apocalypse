Open questions

The inputs that reorder the ranking are the ones worth paying to settle.

  • Where smoke goes. An open source-receptor table for India, by district, sector and season. It fixes the {{ a.smoke.fold }}-fold gap above and every regional measure with it.
  • What the top two cost in full. Ending waste burning and controlling construction dust rank first on an enforcement budget. Someone has to count collection, compliance and treatment.
  • How much the stubble measures cut. Payments not to burn and subsidised machines, measured at scale against monitors.
  • How much paving and sweeping cut road dust, measured in an Indian city. The transport and dust effects here are borrowed from Delhi.
  • Which source study is right. One method, a model and a receptor study side by side, in two seasons, starting with Bengaluru.
  • Counts for the other forty-nine cities. Most measures are scored in one city because the per-city number they need, generator capacity or kiln counts, has not been published.

The notebook

India’s air kills on the scale that tobacco killed in the twentieth century. The response has mostly been theatre: towers that clean a two-hundred-metre radius, aircraft seeding clouds that aren’t there.

This is an open notebook for the unglamorous part — measuring what’s actually happening, finding the analyses nobody has run, and working out which ambitious bets survive contact with arithmetic. Everything is reproducible from free data, and the code is public.

What Diwali does to the air

Ten years of hourly government monitor data for India’s ten largest cities, with the things that happen at the same time pulled apart: the start of winter smog season, crop-stubble smoke, and monsoon rain.

Every city has a real Diwali effect — about four times normal PM2.5 at midnight, and close to twelve times in Chennai. But the famous post-Diwali smog is mostly not Diwali. Adjusted for season, weather and fires, the week after is statistically indistinguishable from normal everywhere. What looks like a hangover is the smog season arriving on schedule.

Confounding runs in opposite directions across the country. In the north, Diwali lands as stubble burning peaks, so a naive comparison overstates it — Delhi falls from +66% to +32%. In the south, retreating monsoon rain washes the air, so a naive comparison understates it: Bengaluru rises from +19% to +35%.

There’s a twist in what people search for. Around Diwali, Indian searches for “air quality index” rise 237% and “air pollution” 36% — while searches for cough and sore throat don’t move at all. Burns rise 75%, so the method can see a real effect when one is there. Attention spikes; symptoms, at least as search reveals them, don’t.

The question behind all of it

concentration ≈ emissions ÷ (mixing height × ventilation)

Every intervention attacks one of those three terms, or else gives up on the air and protects the lungs directly. Sorting proposals by which term they touch kills most of them in a single step.

Two numbers do most of the work. Delhi’s smog weighs about 150 tonnes — the entire visible apocalypse over thirty million people is a few hundred tonnes of dust, which is why scrubbing feels tractable. And it refills in about a day. A perfect, free, instantaneous scrub of the whole city buys one clean day. That ratio kills smog towers, outdoor filtration, and most of what gets announced each November.

Winter cloud seeding fails for a reason worth stating precisely: at fifteen percent humidity, condensing every molecule of water in a kilometre-high column over Delhi yields 2.6 mm of rain. Real efficiency is a fraction of that — drizzle, which doesn’t scrub. It isn’t a technology limit. The water isn’t there.
